Ask what a library leader is supposed to protect, and the answers you get most often are a building, a budget, and a collection. Renovate the space. Defend the headcount. Preserve the archive. Those are real responsibilities. None of them is the job.

---

## The space problem

A lot of library leadership vision starts and ends with square footage. Renovate the ground floor into a maker space, add a café, brand a wing as a "third place." Spaces matter, and a good one draws people in. But a vision built around space treats the library as a destination rather than a participant. It measures success in gate counts and renovation photos instead of in what got discovered, built, or published because the library was in the room. A space is a container. Leadership that stops at the container has confused the shape of the thing for what the thing does.

## The museum problem

The other default vision is custodial: the library as steward of a permanent collection, protecting what already exists. That instinct is not wrong, it is incomplete. Stewarding the scholarly record matters. But an institution that only stewards what already exists has nothing to say about what gets created next. The library's most valuable seat is not in the archive, it is in the research partnership, the instruction collaboration, the infrastructure decision, at the point where new knowledge is actually being made. A museum protects the past. Leadership has to also build the conditions for what comes after it.

## The bean-counter problem

Underneath both of those sits a third pattern that is not really about libraries at all. Higher education broadly has absorbed a management style built for cutting cost and counting output, and library leadership has absorbed it too: efficiency ratios, consolidation, headcount as the primary lever. That style is not rooted in any evolved sense of what the mission is now. It optimizes an org chart against a budget line without asking what capability the actual work of knowledge creation requires today. A library run like a cost center will be run efficiently and will not lead anything.

## The cargo-cult problem

Layered on top of all three is a pattern of following structural fashion instead of function. The profession periodically reorganizes itself around whatever the trend is, functional versus subject specialization being one recurring version, without asking what specific capability the new structure is supposed to produce that the old one couldn't. A structure copied from somewhere else because it is currently in fashion is not a strategy. It is a costume.

## The irony

Here is the part that should embarrass the field more than it does. Librarianship describes itself as grounded in information science, and yet library leadership is frequently the most reluctant part of the institution to adopt new information technology, treating each wave as a threat to manage rather than a competency to claim. That reluctance is not caution. Caution would look like informed adoption on the field's own terms. What it actually looks like is a profession that has stopped believing its own credentials. Metadata, knowledge organization, information architecture, controlled vocabularies, the entire discipline of making information findable and trustworthy: those are not adjacent to what AI and open science need right now. They are exactly what is needed, and the field that has spent a century building that expertise keeps acting like it belongs to someone else.

---

## The claim worth making

Library leadership's actual job is stewarding how knowledge gets created, organized, and sustained, not the room it happens in, not the objects it already produced, not the org chart around it. That means showing up as a research partner, not a landlord. It means treating instruction, data infrastructure, and open science as core mission, not adjacent service. It means leading with the field's real expertise instead of importing someone else's management vocabulary to feel legitimate.

That is a harder vision to build toward than a renovated lobby, and it is the only one that matches what the profession actually knows how to do.
