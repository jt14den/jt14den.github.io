# Validation request: "Library Leadership Is Stewarding the Wrong Thing"

## Context

This is a draft essay for a personal blog (tim-dennis.com) written by a
Director of a university library's Data Science Center — a research-data-
infrastructure and open-science role, not a traditional librarian track. The
site is blog-first, evidence-heavy, and skeptical of AI-fluent-sounding
conclusions; readers are academic library staff, research-computing people,
and open-science/OSPO folks.

This is the 4th essay in a "library leadership" series. The first three
(not included here) argue: programs need to outlast their founders; research
data infrastructure requires specific things from library leadership; eight
years teaching workshops evolved into building research infrastructure. All
three are institutional-critique-register pieces — they argue about what the
field should do, not about the author's personal career story.

**Already decided, don't relitigate:** the essay is publishing as part of
this series (that's settled). The four "anti-pattern" categories below were
deliberately chosen by the author from his own observation of the field —
don't suggest replacing them with a different taxonomy.

## The essay in full

> Ask what a library leader is supposed to protect, and the answers you get
> most often are a building, a budget, and a collection. Renovate the space.
> Defend the headcount. Preserve the archive. Those are real responsibilities.
> None of them is the job.
>
> **The space problem.** A lot of library leadership vision starts and ends
> with square footage. Renovate the ground floor into a maker space, add a
> café, brand a wing as a "third place." Spaces matter, and a good one draws
> people in. But a vision built around space treats the library as a
> destination rather than a participant. It measures success in gate counts
> and renovation photos instead of in what got discovered, built, or
> published because the library was in the room. A space is a container.
> Leadership that stops at the container has confused the shape of the thing
> for what the thing does.
>
> **The museum problem.** The other default vision is custodial: the library
> as steward of a permanent collection, protecting what already exists. That
> instinct is not wrong, it is incomplete. Stewarding the scholarly record
> matters. But an institution that only stewards what already exists has
> nothing to say about what gets created next. The library's most valuable
> seat is not in the archive, it is in the research partnership, the
> instruction collaboration, the infrastructure decision, at the point where
> new knowledge is actually being made. A museum protects the past.
> Leadership has to also build the conditions for what comes after it.
>
> **The bean-counter problem.** Underneath both of those sits a third
> pattern that is not really about libraries at all. Higher education
> broadly has absorbed a management style built for cutting cost and
> counting output, and library leadership has absorbed it too: efficiency
> ratios, consolidation, headcount as the primary lever. That style is not
> rooted in any evolved sense of what the mission is now. It optimizes an
> org chart against a budget line without asking what capability the actual
> work of knowledge creation requires today. A library run like a cost
> center will be run efficiently and will not lead anything.
>
> **The cargo-cult problem.** Layered on top of all three is a pattern of
> following structural fashion instead of function. The profession
> periodically reorganizes itself around whatever the trend is, functional
> versus subject specialization being one recurring version, without asking
> what specific capability the new structure is supposed to produce that
> the old one couldn't. A structure copied from somewhere else because it
> is currently in fashion is not a strategy. It is a costume.
>
> **The irony.** Here is the part that should embarrass the field more than
> it does. Librarianship describes itself as grounded in information
> science, and yet library leadership is frequently the most reluctant part
> of the institution to adopt new information technology, treating each
> wave as a threat to manage rather than a competency to claim. That
> reluctance is not caution. Caution would look like informed adoption on
> the field's own terms. What it actually looks like is a profession that
> has stopped believing its own credentials. Metadata, knowledge
> organization, information architecture, controlled vocabularies, the
> entire discipline of making information findable and trustworthy: those
> are not adjacent to what AI and open science need right now. They are
> exactly what is needed, and the field that has spent a century building
> that expertise keeps acting like it belongs to someone else.
>
> **The claim worth making.** Library leadership's actual job is stewarding
> how knowledge gets created, organized, and sustained, not the room it
> happens in, not the objects it already produced, not the org chart around
> it. That means showing up as a research partner, not a landlord. It means
> treating instruction, data infrastructure, and open science as core
> mission, not adjacent service. It means leading with the field's real
> expertise instead of importing someone else's management vocabulary to
> feel legitimate.
>
> That is a harder vision to build toward than a renovated lobby, and it is
> the only one that matches what the profession actually knows how to do.

## What I want stress-tested

Don't tell me it's well-written. Find where the argument would break under
challenge from a skeptical library-leadership reader, and rank the issues by
whether they'd survive contact with a real critic.

1. **Strawman risk.** Does this attack a real, current, fairly-characterized
   set of behaviors in the field, or a dated/generic version of "bad
   leadership" that could be pasted onto almost any sector? Name the
   specific places where the critique is too generic to land.

2. **The cargo-cult example is unsupported.** The "functional versus subject
   specialization" line is the only concrete example in the whole essay, and
   it's one clause with no named institution, decision, or outcome attached.
   Does the argument need a real example here to be credible, or does the
   essay's manifesto register make that unnecessary? If it needs one, what
   would a defensible example look like without turning into a subtweet of
   a specific institution or person?

3. **Missing evidence, by the author's own standard.** This author's other
   writing on this site is citation-heavy (peer-reviewed papers, named
   sources, primary documents). This essay has zero citations or named
   examples anywhere. Is that a deliberate and fine register shift for a
   manifesto-style piece, or does it undercut the essay's credibility next
   to the rest of the site?

4. **The empirical claim in "The irony" is asserted, not shown.** "Library
   leadership is frequently the most reluctant part of the institution to
   adopt new information technology" is a strong, falsifiable claim stated
   with no evidence. Does it need a hedge, a citation, or a named
   counterexample check? What's the strongest version of a rebuttal a
   library-IT leader could offer?

5. **Redundancy across the five sections.** Are "space," "museum,"
   "bean-counter," and "cargo-cult" four genuinely distinct failure modes,
   or do two of them collapse into the same underlying problem restated?
   If any pair is redundant, which one is weaker and could be cut or
   merged?

6. **The framing decision.** The author is deciding between two versions of
   this essay: (a) institutional critique only, as written — matches the
   register of the other three essays in the series; or (b) the same
   critique plus an added personal angle — why the standard librarian
   career track doesn't produce leaders who think this way, drawing on the
   author's own atypical path into library leadership through
   research-data-infrastructure work rather than traditional librarianship.
   Which version is the stronger essay, and what does the personal angle
   risk (e.g., reading as self-justifying, or diluting the institutional
   argument) versus what it could add (credibility, a concrete throughline
   for why this critique is correct)?

Give me a confidence score (low/medium/high) on whether this essay is ready
to publish as-is, and the two or three concrete edits that would most
improve it if I only had time for a few.

## Outcome (2026-08-16)

External model response (GPT) came back with confidence 30/100 as-is, 90/100
confidence in its own assessment, 85/100 after three focused revisions.

**Verified before adopting anything:**
- Ithaka S+R *US Library Survey 2025* — real, all 7 cited numbers (67/100
  service-orientation mean, 98%/89% research & instruction priority, 31%
  campus-decision involvement, 24%→14% AI non-use, 83% AI-literacy demand,
  ~⅓ AI/ML hiring) confirmed exact against the primary source via WebFetch.
- Hoodless & Pinfield, *JLIS* 2018, "Subject vs. functional" — real, matches
  description (institutional-alignment rationale vs. lost departmental
  relationships).
- Palumbo, Bussmann & Kern, *C&RL* 2021, "The Value of Subject
  Specialization" — real, matches description (200+ science librarians,
  functional roles growing but not replacing liaison relationships).

No fabricated evidence this round — unlike some past external passes, every
load-bearing claim checked out.

**Adopted (applied to the essay):**
1. "Most reluctant part of the institution" (unsupported, contradicted by
   the survey's actual adoption numbers) → replaced with an agency-not-speed
   argument.
2. Opening characterization read as a stereotype against current survey
   data → added a concession paragraph citing the Ithaka survey, relocating
   the essay's real target to the gap between stated priorities and what
   gets protected when budgets tighten.
3. "None of them is the job" (false choice — responsibilities are real) →
   "But they are means, not the mission."
4. Cargo-cult paragraph attacked the wrong target (functional restructuring
   itself, when literature shows it's often reasoned) → rewritten around a
   diagnostic test (named service failure, relationship map, evaluation
   plan) instead of the structural choice.
6. Four anti-patterns are distinct (their own verdict: high survival) →
   added one transition sentence naming them as four substitutions for
   strategy, and reframed "the irony" as a consequence of the other four
   rather than a fifth accusation.
7. "A museum protects the past" was factually thin (museums/archives do far
   more) → "A vision that stops at custody protects the past."
8. Two self-consistency line edits: "stewarding how knowledge gets created"
   → "stewarding the conditions under which knowledge can be created,
   organized, accessed, and sustained" (avoids implying authority over
   researchers' own work); "exactly what is needed" → "indispensable parts
   of what is needed" (essay was reproducing the overclaim it criticizes).

**Deferred, not applied:**
5. Bean-counter section genericness — flagged as real but lower-stakes;
   left as-is.
9. Institutional-only vs. personal-track framing — external model
   recommended institutional-only (matches the other three essays in the
   series, avoids reading as "traditional librarians can't lead this, but I
   can"). Left as Tim's call; no personal-track content added.

Frontmatter `description` updated to match the "means, not mission" /
"conditions under which knowledge gets made" reframing. Still `status:
draft` — one more read before publishing.
